# **APUSH Notes — Jeffersonian Era to the Market Revolution**

# **Marbury v. Madison (1803) & Judicial Review**

- Occurred during the Jeffersonian Years, under President Thomas Jefferson (1801–1809).
- The case centered on the constitutionality of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which had granted certain powers to the courts that the Constitution itself didn’t explicitly allow.
- At the end of John Adams’s presidency, he appointed several “midnight judges” under the Federalist-controlled Congress, but some of the appointment letters were not delivered before Jefferson took office.
- William Marbury, one of these appointees, sued Secretary of State James Madison for refusing to deliver his commission.
- Chief Justice John Marshall ruled that Marbury had the right to his commission but that the Court could not enforce it, because the provision of the Judiciary Act granting that power was unconstitutional.
- This decision established Judicial Review — the Supreme Court’s authority to determine whether laws or acts of Congress violate the Constitution.
- Judicial Review strengthened the judiciary by placing the Constitution above all laws, ensuring the Court could invalidate unconstitutional legislation.

**Law Hierarchy**

Constitution → Federal acts, laws, and treaties → State acts and laws → Local ordinances.

This hierarchy confirmed federal supremacy while still balancing power among branches.

# **War of 1812 (1812–1815)**

- The War of 1812 was fought between the United States and Great Britain.
- Causes:
    - Impressment of American sailors — British naval forces captured American sailors and forced them into service on British ships.
    - Britain’s ongoing war with Napoleon’s France limited trade, and both powers interfered with neutral U.S. shipping.
    - Rising Anglophobia (anti-British sentiment) in America.
    - British trade restrictions hurt the U.S. economy, and Britain tried to control American trade routes.
    - Many Americans, especially “War Hawks,” desired the conquest of Canada to expand the Agrarian Republic envisioned by Jefferson.
    - The British supported Native American resistance, notably Tecumseh’s Confederacy, which sought to prevent American westward expansion.
- 
- William Henry Harrison defeated Tecumseh’s forces at the Battle of Tippecanoe (1811), breaking Native resistance in the Northwest.
- At the war’s start, the U.S. Army was small and underfunded, as much of the nation’s resources were focused on fighting Napoleon indirectly through trade and embargoes.
- When Napoleon was defeated in 1814, Britain redirected military attention to America, intensifying the conflict.
- Battle of New Orleans (January 1815): U.S. forces under Andrew Jackson achieved a major victory against the British.
    - The battle actually occurred after the Treaty of Ghent (December 1814) had been signed, but before the news reached America.
    - It elevated Jackson to national prominence as a war hero.
- 
- Outcome:
    - No territory changed hands — the war ended in a stalemate.
    - However, it confirmed the United States’ existence as an independent nation and fostered strong national pride.
    - The U.S. saw the rise of manufacturing and a temporary decline of Native resistance in the Old Northwest.

# **The American System (1816–1830s)**

- Proposed by Henry Clay, the American System aimed to make the United States economically self-sufficient and reduce reliance on foreign goods.
- It had three main components:
    1. Federal Bank – Re-chartering the Bank of the United States to stabilize currency and credit.
    2. Protective Tariffs – Designed to protect American industry from foreign competition.
    3. Internal Improvements (Infrastructure) – Roads, canals, and later railroads to connect regions for trade and growth.
- 
- The system tied together the North’s industry, South’s agriculture, and West’s resources, promoting interdependence.
- Although controversial (especially the tariffs), it laid the foundation for modern federal economic policy and early industrialization.

# **The Second Party System (1828–1854)**

**Democratic-Republicans (later Democrats)**

- Key Figures: Andrew Jackson, John C. Calhoun, Martin Van Buren.
- Emerged as a populist reaction to elite politics, favoring the “common man.”
- Beliefs:
    - Strong presidency but limited federal government power in domestic affairs.
    - Most authority should rest with states and local institutions.
    - Anti-elite — opposed monopolies, privilege, and the concentration of wealth.
    - Promoted the interests of farmers and laborers, not industrialists.
    - Pro-agriculture and anti-tariff, viewing tariffs as harmful to southern farmers and western settlers.
    - More dominant in the South and West, emphasizing personal liberty and expansionism.
- 

**Whigs (1833–1854)**

- The Whig Party took its name from the British anti-monarchy party, symbolizing opposition to what they saw as “King Andrew Jackson.”
- Key Figures: Henry Clay, Daniel Webster, John Quincy Adams, William Henry Harrison.
- Beliefs:
    - Supported a strong federal government and the American System.
    - Advocated for economic modernization and moral reform.
    - Favored federal involvement in infrastructure and education.
    - Attracted support from urban professionals, northern manufacturers, and reformers.
    - The issue of slavery split and ultimately destroyed the Whig Party by the 1850s, leading many members to join the new Republican Party.

# **The Market Revolution (1790s–1850s)**

The Market Revolution was a massive economic transformation in the early 19th century, characterized by the shift from local economies to a national market.

Technological innovation, infrastructure development, and changing labor systems redefined American society and gender roles.

**Mary Paul’s Letter (1845)**

- Describes her experience working in the Lowell Textile Mills of Massachusetts.
- She wrote to her father about the demanding factory work but also expressed gratitude for employment and independence.
- Illustrates the rise of industrial labor, especially for young women, and the mixed emotions of empowerment and exploitation during industrialization.

**Moses Brown’s Letter on the Textile Industry (1790)**

- Brown helped finance the first successful textile mill in America in Pawtucket, Rhode Island, with Samuel Slater.
- His letter marks the beginning of industrial manufacturing in the U.S., setting a precedent for large-scale factory production and economic infrastructure.

**John Deere’s Steel Plow (1837)**

- Invented to handle the thick, heavy soil of Illinois and the Midwest.
- The solid steel blade was far more effective than cast-iron plows.
- Allowed farmers to cultivate more land efficiently, fueling westward expansion and agricultural productivity during the Market Revolution.

**Baltimore and Ohio Railroad (1828)**

- One of the first major railroads in the U.S., symbolizing the federal government’s support for new transportation infrastructure.
- Railroads connected eastern factories to western markets, accelerating migration, commerce, and national integration.

**Erie Canal (Completed 1825)**

- Connected the Great Lakes to the Atlantic Ocean via the Hudson River.
- Reduced shipping costs and time, spurring economic growth and the creation of industrial towns.
- Became a symbol of America’s commitment to progress and modernization during the Market Revolution.

**Charity Bryant & Sylvia Drake Writings (Early 1800s)**

- Their relationship — recorded through letters and diaries — offers insight into early same-sex relationships and women’s private lives.
- Demonstrates how the era’s social norms and gender roles began to evolve as women sought more independence and personal freedom.

**Edward Everett & the Working Men’s Party (1820s–1830s)**

- Advocated for workers’ rights, fair wages, and education for the working class.
- Highlighted the growing divide between laborers and elites in the new industrial economy.
- Early example of organized labor activism in America.

**Lowell Labor Reform Association (1844)**

- Formed by women working in the Lowell mills to demand better working conditions, shorter hours, and fair pay.
- Reflected the emergence of labor reform movements and women’s early political organization in industrial America.

**Lydia Maria Child’s Writings (1830s–1840s)**

- Author and reformer who wrote “The American Frugal Housewife.”
- Advised women to live modestly and criticized materialism, revealing women’s domestic roles and moral influence in the growing market economy.

**St. Louis: Rebecca Burland (1831)**

- Described her family’s migration to New Orleans and the hardships of settling in the Midwest.
- Her story highlights the challenges of westward migration and the hope for new economic opportunities in a growing America.

**Cincinnati: Timothy Flint’s Accounts (1820s)**

- Writings depicted rapid urbanization and opportunity in new western cities like Cincinnati.
- Encouraged European immigration and illustrated the dynamism of frontier urban centers.

**Philadelphia: An Account (1830s)**

- Described the rise of manufacturing and job opportunities in an increasingly urban and industrial city.
- Represents how the Market Revolution concentrated wealth and labor in growing urban hubs.

**Dorr Rebellion (1841–1842, Rhode Island)**

- Reform movement led by Thomas Wilson Dorr, demanding broader voting rights and a new state constitution.
- The rebellion opposed Rhode Island’s outdated charter government that limited suffrage to property owners.
- Led to the drafting of a new, more democratic constitution and reflected the period’s push for political participation.

**Lowell Mill Women (1830s–1840s)**

- The Lowell Mill Girls were female factory workers in Massachusetts who worked under harsh conditions.
- Their writings and strikes against low wages and poor treatment revealed changing gender roles and early labor consciousness.
- They helped lay the foundation for later women’s and labor rights movements.

# **Trail of Tears (1838–1839)**

- The forced removal of the Cherokee, Creek, Chickasaw, Choctaw, and Seminole peoples from their homelands in the Southeast to reservations west of the Mississippi River.
- Ordered under the Indian Removal Act (1830) signed by Andrew Jackson.
- Despite Supreme Court rulings (like Worcester v. Georgia) that favored the Cherokee, Jackson ignored the Court and enforced removal.
- Over 4,000 Native Americans died during the journey due to exposure, starvation, and disease.
- Even many U.S. soldiers involved expressed shame and guilt over the suffering they witnessed.
- The bill passed Congress by only one vote, symbolizing the deep divisions in American politics over Native rights and federal authority.

# **Nullification Crisis (1832–1833)**

- Centered on President Andrew Jackson’s response to South Carolina’s opposition to federal tariffs.
- Southern states, especially South Carolina, viewed tariffs (like the Tariff of Abominations, 1828) as unfairly favoring Northern manufacturers.
- South Carolina, led by John C. Calhoun, claimed states could nullify federal laws deemed unconstitutional — a revival of the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions (1798) idea.
- Jackson rejected this challenge to federal power, threatening to use force to preserve the Union.
- Congress passed a Compromise Tariff (1833) proposed by Henry Clay, which gradually reduced tariff rates and averted civil war.
- The crisis reinforced federal supremacy over states while deepening sectional tensions that foreshadowed the Civil War.

# **DBQ Prep: Historical Writing Skills**

To succeed on APUSH Document-Based Questions (DBQs), always structure responses with clear, evidence-driven analysis.

| **Skill** | **Point Range** | **What to Include** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thesis | 0–1 | Create a defensible argument answering the prompt directly. |
| Contextualization | 0–1 | Provide broader historical background leading into the topic. |
| Evidence Beyond Documents | 0–1 | Add one piece of relevant evidence not found in the documents. |
| Document Analysis | 0–2 | Analyze point of view, purpose, historical situation, and audience for several documents. |
| Complexity | 0–1 | Connect different themes, causes, and perspectives to show deeper understanding. |

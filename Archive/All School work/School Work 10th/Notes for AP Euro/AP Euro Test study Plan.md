# AP European History Study Plan: April 28 – May 4

**Overview:** This 7-day study plan (April 28–May 4) balances content review and active practice to prep you for the AP European History exam. Each day focuses on a major era or theme, with an emphasis on active recall (self-quizzing, flashcards) and writing practice (DBQs/LEQs). *Plan on ~1–2 hours per day:* if you only have ~1 hour, prioritize the core review and at least outline the essay; if you have closer to 2 hours, complete all tasks and deepen your review. (Studies show that **active recall** techniques like self-testing boost exam performance far more than passive reading ([Active Recall: The Most Effective High-Yield Learning Technique - Osmosis Blog](https://www.osmosis.org/blog/active-recall-the-most-effective-high-yield-learning-technique#:~:text=Wrong,memory%20retention%20and%20academic%20performance)) ([Active Recall: The Most Effective High-Yield Learning Technique - Osmosis Blog](https://www.osmosis.org/blog/active-recall-the-most-effective-high-yield-learning-technique#:~:text=As%20mentioned%20previously%2C%20active%20recall,evidence%20how%20students)), so resist just rereading notes—actively **quiz yourself** instead!) Also, since the free-response section is the toughest part of the AP Euro exam, you’ll practice DBQ and LEQ writing throughout the week (experts recommend lots of essay practice in the weeks leading up to the exam ([Guide to the AP® European History Exam – Marco Learning](https://marcolearning.com/guide-to-the-ap-european-history-exam/#:~:text=The%20free,advanced%20skills%20about%20how%20to))). Adjust the plan as needed for your schedule, and keep track of any topics that need extra attention the next day. Let’s dive into the daily breakdown:

## Monday, April 28 – **Protestant Reformation & Active Recall**

- **Content Review (Reformation):** Actively review the **Protestant Reformation** and early religious revolutions. Without looking at your notes, **brain-dump** key points: Why did Martin Luther challenge the Catholic Church? What were his main ideas (e.g. *sola fide*, *sola scriptura*)? Who were other reformers like John Calvin and Henry VIII, and how did their movements differ? Jot down everything you remember about these figures, the Catholic Counter-Reformation (Council of Trent, Jesuits, etc.), and the impacts of the Reformation (religious wars, rise of Protestant sects) – *then check your notes or textbook to fill any gaps*. This active recall approach will strengthen your memory (far more than just rereading ([Active Recall: The Most Effective High-Yield Learning Technique - Osmosis Blog](https://www.osmosis.org/blog/active-recall-the-most-effective-high-yield-learning-technique#:~:text=Wrong,memory%20retention%20and%20academic%20performance))). If you notice weak spots (say, you forgot Calvin’s idea of predestination or the outcomes of the Peace of Westphalia), highlight those for a quick re-read. *(Approx. 30–40 min)*
- **LEQ Practice (Compare Luther & Calvin):** Practice a long-essay question focused on the Reformation. **Outline an LEQ comparing Martin Luther and John Calvin** – their doctrines, their leadership, and their impacts on European society. For example: “**Compare and contrast the theological ideas and influence of Martin Luther and John Calvin in the Reformation.**” Sketch out a quick essay plan: write a one-sentence **thesis** that answers the prompt, then bullet-point 2–3 **body paragraph topics** (e.g. *salvation and authority* – Luther’s *faith alone* vs. Calvin’s *predestination*; *church structure* – Luther’s reform within state churches vs. Calvin’s theocracy in Geneva; *legacy* – Lutheran spread in Germany/Scandinavia vs. Calvinist influence in Switzerland, Netherlands, Scotland). Under each, list a few specific pieces of **evidence** (e.g. 95 Theses, Luther’s translation of Bible, Calvin’s *Institutes*, Huguenots in France) and how they support your comparison. If you have time, **write out a full intro paragraph** and one body paragraph to practice framing your comparison and using evidence. *Keep this to ~30 minutes for an outline (or ~45–50 min if writing paragraphs).*
    - *Time management:* If you only have an hour today, do the outline and thesis. If you have more time (or want extra practice), try writing the **full LEQ essay** in ~40 minutes as if in exam conditions. Either way, focus on clear organization and a direct comparison of Luther vs. Calvin. After outlining/writing, **self-assess**: Did your thesis answer the prompt? Did you include **contextualization** (e.g. mention how the Renaissance or church corruption set the stage for the Reformation) and specific examples? Adjust your approach based on this self-check for next time.
- **Active Recall Aids:** Finish by testing yourself on key **terms and people** from this era using flashcards or a quick quiz. For example, ensure you can identify: *indulgences*, *95 Theses (1517)*, *Diet of Worms*, *Predestination*, *Jesuits*, *Council of Trent*, *Henry VIII’s Act of Supremacy*, etc. Quiz yourself (or have someone quiz you) on 5–10 terms – speak out the definition or significance **from memory**, then check if correct. This kind of self-testing with flashcards is proven to improve retention ([Active recall strategies associated with academic achievement in young adults: A systematic review - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38461899/#:~:text=Results%3A%20%20Among%20the%20appraised,utilized.%20Concept%20mapping%20was)). *(10–15 min)*
    - *If time permits:* Do a brief **recap of the Renaissance** as context. Try to list 3 ways the Renaissance (think humanism, Erasmus’ critique of the Church, etc.) set the groundwork for the Reformation. This will reinforce your understanding of continuity between periods.

## Tuesday, April 29 – **Absolutism, Enlightenment & Causation**

- **Content Review (17th–18th Centuries):** Today, focus on the Age of **Absolutism** and the **Enlightenment**. Start with absolutism: from memory, summarize what **absolute monarchy** means and recall at least two exemplars (e.g. *Louis XIV* in France – “I am the state,” Versailles centralizing power; and *Peter the Great* in Russia – westernization, control of nobility). Contrast this with **constitutionalism** in England (the *Glorious Revolution* and English Bill of Rights establishing Parliament’s power). Next, jump to the Enlightenment: list **Enlightenment thinkers** and their key ideas without notes. For instance: *John Locke* – natural rights and government by consent; *Voltaire* – free speech and religious tolerance; *Montesquieu* – separation of powers; *Rousseau* – social contract and general will; *Mary Wollstonecraft* – women’s rights. After writing what you recall, cross-check with your notes/textbook and fill in details (names of works like Locke’s *Two Treatises*, Montesquieu’s *Spirit of Laws*, etc.). Make sure you understand how these ideas challenged old institutions (divine-right monarchy, state churches) and sowed seeds for future revolutions. *(30 min)*
    - As you review, practice **active recall** by explaining concepts aloud: e.g. *“How did Louis XIV exemplify absolutism? Why did Enlightenment philosophers criticize absolute government?”* If you find yourself stuck on a concept (say, you can’t remember what *Voltaire* argued), mark it for one more quick read. This process flags areas needing a second look.
- **Practice Writing (Thesis & Outline):** Develop your essay skills on a causation/comparison prompt connecting absolutism and Enlightenment ideas. **Practice writing a strong thesis and brief outline** for a potential LEQ such as: *“To what extent did Enlightenment ideas challenge the institutions of absolutist rule in Europe?”* or *“Analyze the causes of the French Revolution, considering the influence of Enlightenment ideals and the failures of absolutist regimes.”* Pick one prompt and spend ~5 minutes crafting a one- or two-sentence **thesis** that directly answers it (for the second prompt, e.g.: “Enlightenment ideals of liberty and equality undermined the legitimacy of absolutist monarchies, and when combined with the fiscal crises and social inequalities under Louis XVI’s rule, they ignited the French Revolution”). Then take ~15 minutes to **outline** how you’d support this: jot down 2–3 main points or paragraphs. For instance, for the French Revolution prompt: (1) Enlightenment critiques of authority (Locke, Rousseau inspiring demands for rights); (2) absolutist failures – royal debt, inequality (Louis XVI, tax system, estates); (3) the short-term trigger (famine, financial crisis). Under each, list evidence (e.g. Locke’s idea of consent, France’s bankrupt treasury and calling of the Estates-General). This exercise isn’t full essay writing, but it’s training you to quickly formulate an argument and organize relevant evidence – crucial skills for both LEQ and DBQ. *(20 min)*
    - *If you have extra time:* Expand one of your outlined body paragraphs into a **full paragraph** with complete sentences and analysis, or even attempt a quick **SAQ (Short-Answer Question)**: for example, “Identify and explain one Enlightenment idea that challenged the established social or political order.” Write 2–3 sentences answering it and check if you included a factual example and significance. This helps with concise historical writing.
- **Flashcards & Quick Quiz:** Use another 15 minutes on **flashcards/active recall** for today’s material. Quiz yourself on terms like *divine right*, *Versailles*, *mercantilism* (Colbert’s policies under Louis XIV), *Glorious Revolution*, and Enlightenment terms (*salons*, *deism*, *encyclopedias*, etc.). Also include a few key **18th-century events** (e.g. *Seven Years’ War*, *American Revolution* influence) to see the broader picture. Remember, **self-testing** is a high-yield strategy – flashcard drills and practice questions will reinforce your memory better than passive review ([Active recall strategies associated with academic achievement in young adults: A systematic review - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38461899/#:~:text=Results%3A%20%20Among%20the%20appraised,utilized.%20Concept%20mapping%20was)). If you stumble on a card (say you forgot what *salons* were), read the explanation, then shuffle that card back in to test yourself again.
    - *Active connection:* Before finishing, make a quick connection to tomorrow’s focus: ensure you know **why the Enlightenment and absolutist regime problems led to the French Revolution.** For example, can you explain in a few sentences how ideas about natural rights and the failures of Louis XVI’s government set the stage by 1789? This will prep your mind for Wednesday.

## Wednesday, April 30 – **French Revolution & Napoleon**

- **Content Review (1789–1815):** Today, deep-dive into the **French Revolution and Napoleonic Era**. Start by sketching a **timeline** from memory of major phases of the French Revolution: 1789 – Estates-General convened and the Tennis Court Oath; July 14, 1789 – Storming of the Bastille; 1792 – France becomes a republic and the radical phase begins; 1793 – Louis XVI executed, Reign of Terror under Robespierre; 1794 – Thermidorian Reaction ends the Terror; 1799 – Napoleon’s coup (18 Brumaire) ends the Directory; 1804 – Napoleon crowns himself Emperor; 1812 – Napoleon’s failed invasion of Russia; 1815 – Napoleon’s defeat at Waterloo and the Congress of Vienna. Write down as many of these as you recall (don’t worry if you don’t get all dates exactly). Now use your notes or a reliable source to **check and fill in** details: e.g. the role of the Declaration of the Rights of Man (1789), the Committee of Public Safety, Napoleon’s reforms (Napoleonic Code, Concordat with the Church), and what Metternich’s Congress of Vienna did (*balance of power*, restoring monarchies). Spend a bit of time understanding the **causes** of the revolution (Enlightenment ideas, France’s financial crisis, social inequalities in the Three Estates) and the **effects** (end of absolute monarchy in France, spread of revolutionary ideals, rise of Napoleon and eventually conservative restoration in 1815). Use active recall by explaining these causes and effects aloud as if teaching someone, then verify from notes. *(30–40 min)*
    - While reviewing, identify any parts of this era that are fuzzy for you. For example, if you’re unsure about what the *Thermidorian Reaction* was or what the *Napoleonic Code* did, take a moment to clarify those from your resources.
- **LEQ Practice (French Revolution):** Write or outline a practice **Long Essay Question** focused on this revolutionary era. Possible prompts: *“****Evaluate the most significant causes of the French Revolution.****”* or *“****To what extent did Napoleon preserve the ideals of the French Revolution?****”* Choose one. If you have ~40 minutes available, **write a full LEQ essay** under exam-like conditions. If tighter on time, create a detailed outline. In either case, start with a clear **thesis** (e.g. “The French Revolution was caused by a convergence of Enlightenment ideological challenges to the Old Regime, a severe financial crisis, and social inequalities that together made the collapse of royal authority inevitable.”). Then structure your argument:
    - For a causes prompt, your body paragraphs might cover (1) Enlightenment and rising expectations (philosophes, American Revolution’s example), (2) economic hardship and mismanagement (debt from wars, inequitable taxation, bread prices), (3) social inequality (Third Estate burdens vs. First/Second Estate privileges, frustration of bourgeoisie).
    - For the Napoleon prompt, paragraphs could address how Napoleon **fulfilled** revolutionary ideals (e.g. Napoleonic Code equalizing laws, merit-based bureaucracy, spread of nationalism) **and** how he **betrayed** them (censorship, crowning himself Emperor, limiting political liberty).
Write with an eye on **specific evidence**: mention events like the calling of the Estates-General, the March on Versailles, or policies like Napoleon’s Concordat with the Pope. Try to include one sentence of **contextualization** in your intro (e.g. “In 1789, after a century of Enlightenment thought and in the aftermath of costly wars like the American Revolution, France found itself...”). Also consider a brief **synthesis/complexity** if you can (for instance, connect to a different revolution or note a long-term consequence in Europe). Time yourself to about 40 minutes of writing.
    - *After writing:* Check your work against the **LEQ rubric** or a checklist: Did you state a thesis and stay focused on it? Did you use **specific historical evidence** (names, dates, documents)? If you have a teacher or peer who can review it, great – otherwise, self-assess critically. Mark anything you forgot or think you could improve (maybe you realized you didn’t mention the role of the middle class – note that for next time). This reflection will help you write a better essay next round.
- **Quick Review & Recall:** Use any remaining time (10–15 minutes) for a quick **self-quiz** on this period and to reinforce prior topics:
    - **Flashcard/Quiz on French Rev:** Quiz yourself on terms/people: *Estates-General*, *Tennis Court Oath*, *Robespierre*, *Reign of Terror*, *Declaration of the Rights of Man*, *Napoleonic Code*, *Continental System*, *Congress of Vienna*. Make sure you can describe each.
    - **Spaced review of earlier topics:** Do a brief mental recap of Monday/Tuesday material to keep it fresh (for example, ask yourself: “What were two differences between Lutheran and Calvinist beliefs?” or “Name one Enlightenment thinker and one idea that influenced the French Revolution.”). This kind of spaced retrieval strengthens memory ([Active recall strategies associated with academic achievement in young adults: A systematic review - PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38461899/#:~:text=Results%3A%20%20Among%20the%20appraised,utilized.%20Concept%20mapping%20was)). If you find you’ve forgotten something from earlier in the week, spend a couple of minutes refreshing it.
    - If you have access to any **practice multiple-choice questions** for the 18th century/Revolutionary period (from a review book or online), try a set of 5–10 questions. This will test your retention in a format similar to the exam’s multiple-choice. Check your answers to see if there are any content areas to brush up on.

## Thursday, May 1 – **19th Century: Industrialization & Nationalism**

- **Content Review (1815–1914):** The 19th century is a big span, so today’s goal is to **survey the major themes**: the Industrial Revolution, the rise of new ideologies (liberalism, socialism, nationalism, etc.), the Revolutions of 1848, and the unification of nations (Italy and Germany), plus imperialism. Start by tackling the **Industrial Revolution**: from memory, list where and why it began (Britain, late 1700s, due to capital, coal, etc.), and at least 3 key inventions or developments (e.g. steam engine, railroad expansion, factory system) and their social effects (urbanization, new factory class of workers *proletariat*, changing family roles). Recall thinkers like *Karl Marx* and *Adam Smith* as responding to industrial capitalism (Marx’s socialism/communism critique vs. Smith’s free-market ideas). Next, think about **political ideologies** and events: define *liberalism* (middle-class suffrage, constitutional government), *conservatism* (tradition, monarchy), *nationalism* (peoples’ desire for nation-states), *romanticism* (cultural movement), etc., and recall that 1848 saw many revolutions across Europe driven by these ideas (France’s 1848 revolution, uprisings in German states, Austria, etc., many of which failed). Now move to **unifications**: without notes, try to outline how Italy unified by 1861 (figures: Cavour, Garibaldi, roles of war against Austria) and how Germany unified by 1871 (Bismarck’s realpolitik, Austro-Prussian War 1866, Franco-Prussian War 1870-71 leading to the German Empire). Lastly, note the late-century **imperialism & colonialism** (the “Scramble for Africa” in the 1880s, Asian spheres of influence) as context that leads into 20th century conflicts. This is a lot, so use a **structured approach**: maybe draw a quick chart or timeline dividing 1815–1848 (post-Napoleonic order and early industrialization), 1848–1871 (revolutions and unifications), 1871–1914 (imperialism, alliances forming). Fill in what you remember, then consult your notes/text to correct and add detail. Focus on understanding **cause and effect**: e.g. how Industrialization caused social changes that led to new ideologies and reforms, or how nationalism led to unification but also sowed rivalry (France vs. Germany after 1871). *(40 min)*
    - Use active recall techniques throughout: for instance, take a blank sheet and try to **sketch Europe’s map** pre- and post-1871 (which major countries existed before vs. after unification? where were Austrian Empire holdings vs. independent nations after?). Or explain aloud “Why did the Revolutions of 1848 happen and why did most fail?” Even if you can’t recall every detail, articulating what you do know helps identify missing pieces when you cross-check later.
- **Practice (Outline or Short Essay):** Choose a 19th-century focused prompt and practice formulating your answer. For example, **outline an LEQ** on one of these:
    - *“Explain two important social or political effects of the Industrial Revolution in Europe.”* – You could discuss (1) the rise of the factory working class and subsequent labor movements/Marxism, and (2) the shift in gender roles/family structure or urbanization leading to public health reforms.
    - *“Compare the process of unification in Germany and Italy.”* – Outline similarities (nationalism, wars led by strong states – Prussia and Piedmont) and differences (Germany’s unification led by Bismarck’s strategic wars, Italy’s more fragmented efforts with multiple leaders like Cavour/Garibaldi).
    - *“Evaluate the causes and outcomes of the Revolutions of 1848 in one country.”* – e.g. pick France or Austrian Empire: cause (widespread discontent: liberal demands + nationalist tensions + economic hardship) vs outcome (initial success like the French Second Republic or Hungarian uprising, but eventual conservative restoration by 1849).
Spend about 20–30 minutes on this exercise. If you feel comfortable, **write one of the body paragraphs** in full to keep your writing skills sharp – for instance, write a paragraph on how industrialization led to the growth of socialism, including examples (Marx/Engels publishing *The Communist Manifesto* in 1848, the formation of workers’ unions, etc.). If you’re outlining, focus on selecting the **best evidence** you’d use and the logical order of points. Practicing organizing these thoughts now will save you time on the actual exam.
    - *Alternate DBQ practice:* If you want a break from LEQs and have access to a set of documents (from a textbook or AP Classroom), you could instead **practice a DBQ skill** today: for example, take a known DBQ prompt like *“Effects of Industrialization on European society”* (a classic topic) and **spend 15 minutes analyzing the documents** without writing the full essay. Jot down for each document: the source/author, its main idea, and how you might use it as evidence. Then outline how you’d group the documents into paragraphs. This targeted practice helps you get faster at decoding docs and planning DBQs. (If you don’t have a specific DBQ, skip this and focus on the LEQ practice above.)
- **Review with Flashcards/Quizzes:** Use the last 15 minutes for quick recall:
    - Run through flashcards or a self-made list of key **19th-century terms**: *Congress of Vienna*, *liberal vs. conservative*, *1848 Revolutions*, *Realpolitik*, *Otto von Bismarck*, *Giuseppe Garibaldi*, *Industrial Revolution*, *urbanization*, *socialism*, *imperialism*. Ensure you can define each or state its significance.
    - **Self-quiz**: Ask yourself a few broad questions, like “What were two consequences of the Industrial Revolution?” or “Why was nationalism a destabilizing force for multi-ethnic empires?” Answer aloud or in writing from memory, then verify with notes.
    - If you have extra time or energy, do a small set of **practice multiple-choice questions** on 19th-century topics. This will not only test your knowledge but also give you practice with stimulus-based questions that AP Euro uses.
    - Finally, glance ahead to tomorrow’s focus (World War I) by recalling how the 19th century set the stage: Can you name the two main alliance blocs (Allies vs Central Powers) forming by the early 20th century? Do you recall the imperial and nationalist tensions (e.g. in the Balkans) that were brewing? Jot down a couple of points as a mental warm-up for WWI.

## Friday, May 2 – **World War I (Causes, Diplomacy & Aftermath)**

- **Content Review (1914–1918):** Shift to the **Great War (World War I)** and its subtle aspects beyond just battle dates. Focus first on the **long-term causes**: try the acronym **MAIN** from memory – *Militarism, Alliances, Imperialism, Nationalism*. For each, recall a specific example: Militarism – European arms race, Germany’s naval build-up; Alliances – the pre-war alliance system (Triple Alliance of Germany/Austria-Hungary/Italy vs. Triple Entente of France/Russia/Britain) and how a localized conflict could trigger a chain reaction; Imperialism – competition for colonies (e.g. crises in Morocco) heightening rivalries; Nationalism – ethnic tensions (Serb nationalism in the Balkans, pan-Slavism challenging Austro-Hungarian Empire). Now add the **spark**: the 1914 assassination of Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo, which led Austria-Hungary to declare war on Serbia, activating the alliance dominoes. Write out this cause-and-effect chain from memory, then check your notes to ensure you’ve got it right. Next, consider **diplomacy and key events during the war**: for instance, the failure of the Schlieffen Plan (Germany’s strategy to knock out France quickly), major turning points like the U.S. entering the war in 1917, and the Russian exit (Treaty of Brest-Litovsk in 1918 after the Russian Revolution, which you’ll review tomorrow). Think about **life beyond the trenches** too: what was happening on the home front? (Total war economy, rationing, women working in factories – e.g. “munitionettes”). Recall any diplomatic attempts or discussions *during* the war (perhaps Woodrow Wilson’s 14 Points in 1918 as a vision for postwar peace, which was an idealistic diplomatic effort). Finally, review the **outcomes**: the armistice on November 11, 1918 and the subsequent **Treaty of Versailles (1919)** – note its key terms (punitive reparations on Germany, loss of territory, League of Nations creation, new nations carved out of empires) and how it aimed to shape diplomacy going forward but also sowed resentment. Use active recall by summarizing *without notes* “What were the three most important results of World War I for Europe?” (e.g. downfall of empires in Germany/Austria-Russia-Ottoman, harsh peace settlement, social changes like lost generation and women’s suffrage movements accelerating). Then confirm with your notes if you missed anything. *(30–40 min)*
    - **Key emphasis:** Make sure you understand the **diplomatic landscape**: how alliances and treaties influenced the war’s outbreak and conclusion. For instance, be clear on why the Alliance system turned a regional conflict into a world war, and how diplomacy at Versailles was as important as military victory in shaping the postwar world. If any of these are unclear, spend a few minutes reading that section in your review book or notes.
- **Practice DBQ (World War I Diplomacy/Effects):** The free-response question you should focus on today is a **Document-Based Question**. Practice with a WWI-themed DBQ to hone your document analysis and essay writing under time constraints. **Choose a DBQ prompt related to World War I** – for example, *“Analyze how World War I affected international diplomacy and European society.”* (If you have an official prompt, use that; the College Board’s website has past AP Euro exam questions you can use ([Guide to the AP® European History Exam – Marco Learning](https://marcolearning.com/guide-to-the-ap-european-history-exam/#:~:text=The%20free,and%20start%20to%20craft%20essays)). You might find a DBQ about, say, the changes in women’s roles due to WWI, or the diplomacy of the peace treaties. Use whatever is available to you that’s WWI-related.) Once you have a prompt and documents:
    - **Plan (15 minutes):** Start your timer and spend up to 15 minutes reading the documents and planning. For each document, note the source/author, the main point, and think about its **point of view or purpose** (is it a government report, a soldier’s letter, a political cartoon? how does that influence its perspective?). Group the documents into 2–3 logical categories for your essay argument (e.g. documents that show wartime diplomatic goals vs. documents that show social changes on the home front). Jot a tentative **thesis** that answers the prompt (for our example, maybe: “World War I fundamentally shifted European diplomacy from balance-of-power jockeying to idealistic but unstable peace efforts, while also accelerating social changes such as expanded roles for women, though these shifts were met with both hopes and deep upheaval”).
    - **Write (45 minutes):** Now spend about 45 minutes **writing the DBQ essay**. Aim to use **most or all of the documents** to support your argument (remember, you need to incorporate *at least six* docs well to maximize points, and try to **sourcing** for at least three of them – e.g. explain a document’s reliability, audience, or point of view in context). Include at least one piece of **outside knowledge** beyond the documents (for example, if the docs don’t mention it, you could bring in “the Christmas Truce of 1914” as an example of soldiers’ shared humanity, or cite the Zimmermann Telegram as a diplomatic incident, or mention the influenza pandemic of 1918 as a societal impact). Keep your eye on the clock; practice finishing within the hour. If you get pressed for time, write bullet-point notes for the remaining paragraphs rather than nothing at all – it’s good to simulate the pressure and learn where you need to speed up.
    - **Review:** After time is up, pause and breathe! Quickly **self-evaluate** your DBQ: Did you clearly state a thesis and address the prompt? Did you use evidence from nearly all docs? Did you attempt POV/sourcing analysis and give outside evidence? Check the DBQ rubric (if you have it) to see which points you think you earned. Jot down a couple of improvements for next time (e.g. “Remember to add a concluding sentence tying back to thesis” or “Spend less time summarizing docs, more time analyzing”). If you have access to sample responses or scoring guidelines, compare them to your essay to gauge where you stand.
    - *Time-management tip:* Writing a full DBQ in 60 minutes is challenging but this practice is invaluable. If you only have ~1 hour total to study today, you might shorten this by outlining the DBQ rather than writing every paragraph. However, **at least practicing the document analysis and thesis formation** is crucial. (Practicing DBQs and LEQs in the lead-up to the exam is one of the best ways to improve ([Guide to the AP® European History Exam – Marco Learning](https://marcolearning.com/guide-to-the-ap-european-history-exam/#:~:text=The%20free,advanced%20skills%20about%20how%20to)).) If you have a full 2 hours today, you could even do another DBQ or look at a second prompt’s documents for more exposure, but don’t burn out – quality over quantity.
- **Quick Debrief & Recall:** End the session by reviewing a few **key facts or terms from WWI** one more time (active recall). For example: *“What did Article 231 (the War Guilt clause) of the Treaty of Versailles state?”*, *“Name one new nation created after WWI”* (Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, etc.), *“What was the League of Nations and one reason it struggled?”* Answer those from memory, then verify. This cements the finer details that might be useful as evidence on the exam.
    - If any WWI-related concept is still murky (maybe you realize you’re unclear on the details of Wilson’s 14 Points vs. the actual Treaty terms), take a brief 5 minutes to clarify that now.

## Saturday, May 3 – **Russian Revolution & Interwar Europe**

- **Content Review (Russian Revolution 1917):** Today, concentrate on the **Russian Revolution** and the interwar period context. Start with the Russian Revolution, as it’s a crucial event often less understood. Try to recount the sequence and causes **out loud or on paper from memory**: Russia in WWI (huge losses, famine, incompetence at the top), the March 1917 *February Revolution* (women’s bread riots in Petrograd, soldiers mutinying, Tsar Nicholas II abdicates – end of Romanov rule), the provisional government under Kerensky (struggling to continue war and reform), then the *Bolshevik (October) Revolution* in October 1917 led by Lenin (slogans “Peace, Land, Bread” promising to withdraw from WWI and redistribute land). Remember what came next: early 1918 Russia exits WWI via the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (ceding territory to Germany), a brutal civil war between Reds (Bolsheviks) and Whites, which the Bolsheviks win by 1921, and the establishment of the **USSR** (Union of Soviet Socialist Republics) under Lenin (and soon after, Lenin’s NEP policy). After writing or speaking through this, check your notes for any missing pieces (e.g. the role of figures like Leon Trotsky, or the execution of the royal family in 1918). Make sure you understand *why* the revolution occurred: Russia’s backward economy and losses in WWI created misery; the Tsar’s inability to reform (no effective sharing of power, Bloody Sunday in 1905’s legacy) eroded support; and Lenin and the Bolsheviks had organization and a clear message that appealed to war-weary, hungry masses. *(20–30 min)*
    - **Interwar (1919–1930s) Review:** Next, do a high-level review of the rest of the interwar period. Key things to recall: the 1920s were a mix of fragile democracies and unresolved tensions. The League of Nations was formed but was weak (the U.S. didn’t join, lack of enforcement power). The mid-1920s had some stability (the “Locarno treaties” easing German-French tensions, and the brief prosperity from 1924-29), but then the **Great Depression (1929)** hit, causing mass unemployment and disillusionment with democratic governments. This paved the way for the rise of **totalitarian regimes**: remember that *Mussolini* took power in Italy in 1922 with Fascism, *Stalin* eventually took over after Lenin (by late 1920s) and started his first Five-Year Plan in 1928, and *Hitler* and the Nazis rose in Germany (Hitler appointed chancellor in 1933 amid economic crisis, then establishing dictatorship). Also note appeasement in the late 1930s: Britain and France’s policy of trying to avoid war by conceding to some of Hitler’s demands (e.g. the 1938 Munich Agreement giving Hitler the Sudetenland). Use active recall by listing: “What were three factors that allowed authoritarian leaders like Hitler and Mussolini to gain support?” (Possible answers: economic crisis/high unemployment, fear of communism, nationalist resentment of Treaty of Versailles, charismatic propaganda). Then verify with notes or a review resource that you’ve got the main points. *(15–20 min)*
    - **Connect to WWII causes:** Ensure you see how these interwar developments lead into World War II: the harshness of Versailles fueling German aggression, the Depression weakening democracies, and aggressive moves by dictators (Japan invading Manchuria 1931, Italy invading Ethiopia 1935, Germany rearming and expanding Rhineland/Austria/Czechoslovakia late ’30s) largely unchecked due to appeasement and isolationism. This understanding will be useful for tomorrow’s review of WWII.
- **LEQ Practice (Russian Revolution or Comparison):** Now, practice a writing task on the Russian Revolution or a comparison involving it:
    - One option is to **outline an LEQ on the causes and effects of the 1917 Russian Revolution.** For example: *“Analyze the major causes of the 1917 Russian Revolution and its impact on European politics in the ensuing decade.”* Write a thesis (e.g. “World War I’s strains, combined with Russia’s longstanding social injustices and an out-of-touch monarchy, sparked the 1917 Russian Revolution, which not only transformed Russia into the world’s first communist state but also sent shockwaves through Europe, inspiring both fear and imitation in the interwar period.”). Then outline a couple of body paragraphs: (1) Causes – war fatigue (army defeats, home front starvation), incompetence of Tsarist regime (e.g. Rasputin scandal, failure of 1905 reforms), appeal of Lenin’s Bolshevik program; (2) Effects – Russia withdraws from WWI (Allies feel betrayed), the emergence of a communist government alarms Western countries (e.g. fear of spread of communism, Red Scare), and it inspires leftist movements elsewhere. Add evidence under each (e.g. casualties in WWI, Rasputin’s influence, Lenin’s April Theses, Comintern formation in 1919, etc.).
    - Another rich practice is a **comparison essay outline**: *“Compare the Russian Revolution of 1917 with the French Revolution of 1789 in terms of causes or outcomes.”* If you choose this, create a brief chart of similarities vs differences. Similar causes: both saw economic hardship (bread riots in Petrograd 1917, bread prices in Paris 1789), unpopular rulers (Nicholas II’s ineptitude vs. Louis XVI’s indecisiveness), influence of ideas (Marxist ideology of class struggle vs. Enlightenment ideals of liberty). Differences: the French Rev started against an **ancient regime** monarchy and eventually produced a liberal-ish order (before Napoleon), whereas the Russian Rev started against an outdated monarchy and produced a radical **Marxist** regime; also, foreign war context was immediate in Russia (WWI), whereas France’s big wars came *after* the revolution began. Sketch these out and think of evidence (French: Estates-General, Declaration of Rights of Man; Russian: Bolshevik slogans, land decrees, etc.).
    - Spend ~30 minutes on the above, writing at least the thesis and a detailed outline. If you have time and energy, try writing one full body paragraph or even timing a full 40-minute essay on one of the prompts. This practice is especially good for making connections across periods (synthesis) and will reinforce content from both revolutions.
- **Active Recall Drill & Flashcards:** Use 10–15 minutes for a final drill on Russian Revolution and interwar facts:
    - Quiz yourself on names and terms: *Lenin*, *Bolsheviks*, *“Peace, Land, Bread”*, *Tsar Nicholas II*, *Kerensky*, *Treaty of Brest-Litovsk*, *Five-Year Plans*, *collectivization*, *Weimar Republic*, *Mussolini*, *Appeasement*, *Munich 1938*. Make sure you can associate each with a short description or significance.
    - Try a quick **teach-back:** Explain the Russian Revolution in 2 minutes as if you’re teaching a friend who knows nothing about it. Cover why it happened and what changed. Teaching is a form of active recall that can really show you if there’s any part you’re shaky on.

## Sunday, May 4 – **World War II & Final Review**

- **Content Review (1939–1945 WWII):** It’s the last day before the exam, so do a focused yet not overtaxing review of **World War II** and its subtler aspects, then a comprehensive wrap-up. Begin with a recap of WWII’s **causes and early diplomacy**: Without notes, list the major steps to war – Hitler’s actions (rearmament, remilitarizing the Rhineland, Anschluss with Austria 1938, demands on Sudetenland leading to the Munich Conference in 1938, then the Nazi-Soviet Nonaggression Pact of August 1939 that shocked the world). Recall the failure of **appeasement**: at Munich, Britain and France let Hitler take the Sudetenland (hoping to satisfy his expansion), but in September 1939 he invaded Poland, which finally sparked war. Note that **World War II** truly begins in September 1939 with the invasion of Poland (causing Britain/France to declare war on Germany). Now outline the war’s big picture (you don’t need every battle): Germany’s early blitzkrieg successes (Poland, then Western Europe in 1940, France falls), Battle of Britain (1940 air war), Hitler’s invasion of the USSR in 1941 (Operation Barbarossa) which eventually fails, the entry of the **United States** after Pearl Harbor (Dec 1941) turning the tide, major turning points like Stalingrad (1942-43) and D-Day (June 1944 opening a Western front), and the final defeat of the Axis in 1945 (Germany surrenders May 1945, Japan in Sept 1945 after atomic bombs). Now focus on the **“subtle” topics beyond battles**: wartime diplomacy among Allies – e.g. the **Tehran Conference** (1943), **Yalta (Feb 1945)** and **Potsdam (July 1945)** where Roosevelt/Churchill/Stalin (later Truman/Attlee) planned post-war arrangements (Yalta decided on Germany’s division, free elections in Eastern Europe – which became contentious; Potsdam occurred when the war in Europe was over but Japan ongoing, setting stage for Cold War tensions). Also note the **Holocaust** and genocide as a crucial part of WWII’s impact – the “Final Solution” drastically changed ethical and human rights consciousness post-war. Think about the war’s **outcomes**: Europe in ruins, the beginning of the **Cold War** (with two superpowers USA vs USSR emerging and dividing Europe ideologically), the United Nations founded in 1945 to promote peace, and colonial empires crumbling in subsequent decades due to the war’s strain and rise of independence movements. After recalling as much as you can, consult your notes or a summary to ensure accuracy, especially on the conferences and treaties. *(30 min)*

Over the course of the seventeenth century, both absolutist and constitutional governments set common goals:

- Protect and expand their frontiers
- Raise new taxes
- Consolidate central control
- Compete for new colonies in the New and Old Worlds

- Delays in conveying orders to the provinces
- Lack of information about their realms
- Local power structures
- Linguistic diversity that limited subjects' willingness to obey commands

### **The Foundations of French Absolutism (CH.15 - Sec 2)**

### **Henry IV and the Stabilization of France**

- **Background:** Henry IV ascended to the throne in 1589, inheriting a fractured France weakened by religious civil wars (between Protestants and Catholics), crop failures, and reduced trade.
- **Policies for Recovery:**
    - **Peace Initiatives:** Henry focused on maintaining internal peace and issued the Edict of Nantes, granting religious tolerance to Protestants (Huguenots), reducing religious tension.
    - **Economic and Infrastructure Reforms:** He lowered taxes for peasants and imposed fees on royal officials to bring in revenue. Henry invested in infrastructure improvements, including roads and bridges, to boost commerce and stabilize the economy.
    - **Legacy:** Henry IV’s assassination in 1610 left a relatively stable foundation for absolutism in France.

### **Cardinal Richelieu’s Reforms (1628)**

- **Domestic Policies:** Richelieu, minister to Louis XIII, strengthened royal control:
    - **Suppressing Protestantism:** Richelieu viewed Protestantism as a divisive force and sought to limit its influence, especially among the nobility.
    - **Intendant System:** He created an administrative system of intendants, royal bureaucrats loyal only to the king, who implemented royal policies and reduced the power of the nobles.
- **Foreign Policies:** Richelieu’s primary goal was to weaken the Habsburgs, who surrounded France on both sides (German and Spanish territories). He pursued *raison d’état* (reason of state) policies, supporting actions beneficial to France’s strength and security, even if they conflicted with his Catholic beliefs.
- **Politique Approach:** Richelieu is seen as a politique, a leader prioritizing political stability over religious unity, as he compromised on religious matters to secure power.

### **Louis XIV and the Rise of Absolutism in France**

### **The Fronde (1648-1653)**

- **Cause:** Under Louis XIV’s regency, Cardinal Mazarin attempted to increase royal revenue to fund wars, leading to noble uprisings known as the Fronde.
- **Effects:** The Fronde caused widespread disorder and economic hardship, leaving a deep impression on the young Louis. As king, Louis XIV committed to never allowing the nobles to threaten royal authority again, setting the stage for absolute control.

### **Louis XIV’s Reign and Centralization of Power**

- **Divine Right of Kings:** Louis XIV, also called the "Sun King," believed in the divine right, viewing himself as God’s appointed ruler answerable only to God, which justified his centralized power.
- **Government Organization:** He used the hierarchy of the divine right to place himself above the law, controlling the administration and judiciary to maintain unquestioned authority. Louis also reinforced his power through patronage of the church to promote his rule.

### **Life at Versailles and Nobility Control**

- **Relocation to Versailles (1682):** Louis moved his court to Versailles, making it the political and cultural center of France. He required nobles to spend part of the year at Versailles, where he monitored and influenced them.
- **“Gilded Cage”:** Louis kept nobles preoccupied with court rituals and honors, reducing the chances of rebellion. Positions at Versailles were highly sought after, ensuring noble loyalty.
- **Women’s Role:** Women at Versailles were instrumental in political life, promoting candidates for honors, shaping policies, and arranging alliances.

### **Patronage of the Arts**

- **Art as a Political Tool:** Louis viewed art as a means to glorify his reign and solidify his legacy. He heavily funded artistic projects that celebrated the monarchy, integrating art and politics to support his absolutist rule.

### **French Economic Policy and Colbert’s Mercantilism**

- **Jean-Baptiste Colbert’s Reforms:** Colbert, Louis’s controller general, applied mercantilist principles, aiming to make France economically self-sufficient and benefit the state.
    - **Mercantilism Defined:** An economic theory focused on maximizing exports and minimizing imports to increase national wealth, particularly in gold and silver.
    - **Specific Policies:** Colbert imposed high tariffs on imports, promoted French industries with subsidies, created monopolies, and improved quality control for goods. He also developed a strong merchant marine for export.
    - **Outcomes:** While Colbert’s policies boosted the French economy and increased state revenue, the economic benefits were short-lived and failed to prevent later financial issues.

### **Louis XIV’s Wars and the War of Spanish Succession**

- **Military Reforms:** François le Tellier, marquis de Louvois, reformed the French army, making it a professional force directly controlled by the state, setting a model for Europe.
- **Expansion Goals:** Louis sought to expand France to its “natural borders,” capturing territories in the Spanish Netherlands, Flanders, and other regions. While initial wars brought gains, later conflicts strained French resources.
- **War of Spanish Succession (1701-1714):**
    - **Cause:** The death of childless King Charles II of Spain led Louis to support his grandson’s claim to the Spanish throne, but other European powers opposed this to prevent a union between France and Spain.
    - **Outcome:** The Treaty of Utrecht ended the war, with Louis agreeing to keep France and Spain separate. However, the war left France deeply indebted and weakened internationally.

### **The Decline of Absolutist Spain in the Seventeenth Century**

### **Economic and Social Issues in Spain**

- **Decline of Silver Wealth:** Spain’s economy depended on silver from the Americas, but depletion of these mines and competition from local industries led to a decline in revenue.
- **Bankruptcies and Inflation:** The crown’s repeated devaluation of currency and bankruptcies caused inflation, which weakened Spain’s economy and diminished its international credit.
- **Labor and Agricultural Decline:** Spain’s nobility upheld a culture that discouraged profitable labor and trade, and the expulsion of skilled Muslim workers (Moriscos) harmed productivity. To maintain their lifestyle, nobles raised rents, driving peasants off their lands and reducing agricultural output.

### **Political and Military Factors Leading to Decline**

- **Military Overreach and Costly Wars:** Spain’s involvement in prolonged conflicts, such as the Thirty Years' War, drained resources. Unable to sustain these efforts, Spain’s political influence in Europe waned as competing powers like France and the Dutch Republic grew stronger.
- **Internal Political Instability:** Ineffective leadership, combined with corruption and reliance on outdated policies, prevented Spain from addressing its declining power effectively.

### **Absolutism in Austria and Prussia (Ch. 15, Sec. 3)**

### Social Conditions of Eastern Europe

- **Serfdom in Eastern Europe vs. Western Europe:** In the west, the Black Death allowed many peasants to escape serfdom as population losses let them acquire land. However, in eastern Europe, nobles responded to labor shortages by increasing restrictions on peasants, tightening their hold on the rural population.
- **Power of the Nobility:** Without strong royal oversight, eastern lords held extensive power over peasants, acting as local judges, jailers, and prosecutors. They controlled peasants’ rights, including marriage, property, and family life, often separating families and demanding labor obligations.
- **Economic Incentives for Serfdom:** Nobles profited from serfdom by maintaining large estates that produced surplus agricultural products, mainly grains and wheat, for export. This economic structure incentivized nobility to keep peasants tied to their lands, ensuring a reliable labor force for agriculture.

### Austrian Habsburgs’ Rise

- **Impact of the Thirty Years' War:** The Habsburgs, weakened and impoverished, shifted focus from imperial dominance to consolidating power in central and eastern Europe. They aimed to unify their lands internally rather than expand westward.
- **Ferdinand II’s Reforms:** Ferdinand II, a Catholic, led a Counter-Reformation across Austria, especially in Bohemia, suppressing Protestantism and consolidating power. Victories during the Thirty Years' War helped Ferdinand centralize authority, reducing regional autonomy.
- **Ferdinand III’s Military Centralization:** Ferdinand III further centralized power by focusing on building a standing army under his control. He introduced economic reforms, emphasizing mercantilism and a centralized military, setting the foundation for a strong, unified Austrian state.

### Prussian Consolidation under Frederick William and Frederick William I

- **Frederick William, “The Great Elector” (1640):** Frederick William unified Brandenburg, Prussia, and the Rhine territories under a centralized government. To maintain control, he imposed high taxes, built a strong army, and established a bureaucracy loyal to him, effectively centralizing administration in Berlin.
- **Frederick William I, “The Soldier King” (1713–1740):** Frederick William I eliminated any remnants of local self-government, creating a rigid, militaristic state. He invested heavily in the Prussian army, made the military the foundation of society, and structured the nobility (Junkers) into a loyal officer class, ensuring centralized control.

### **Absolutism in Russia and the Ottoman Empire (Ch. 15, Sec. 4)**

### The Mongol Influence and the Rise of Moscow

- **Mongol Rule Over Russia:** The Mongols conquered Kievan Rus in the 13th century, forcing the Slavic princes to submit, collect taxes, and provide tribute. This control solidified Moscow’s political power, as Muscovite princes helped suppress uprisings and gain territory, consolidating influence.
- **Ivan III (Ivan the Great, r. 1462–1505):** Ivan III expanded Moscow’s power and, by 1480, declared independence from Mongol rule. He centralized power in Moscow, creating an autonomous principality and setting the stage for future Russian expansion.

### Ivan IV (Ivan the Terrible) and Expansion of the Russian Empire

- **Ivan IV’s Reign (r. 1533–1584):** Crowned as the first tsar, Ivan IV expanded Russian borders, defeated remaining Mongol territories, and began building a multiethnic empire. He targeted the boyars (nobles) through violent campaigns, creating a service nobility, whose loyalty he ensured by redistributing confiscated lands.
- **The Oprichnina:** Ivan implemented the “oprichnina,” a policy that created a territory directly controlled by the tsar, removing land from the boyars and increasing his control over wealthy merchants, effectively making them agents of the tsar.

### The Romanov Dynasty and Peter the Great’s Reforms

- **Michael Romanov’s Rise (r. 1613–1645):** Michael Romanov came to power through the assembly in 1613, beginning the Romanov dynasty after the “Time of Troubles” – a period marked by instability following Ivan IV’s death. Despite reforms, internal rebellions continued due to inequality and serfdom.
- **Peter the Great (r. 1682–1725):** Determined to modernize Russia, Peter reformed the military, centralized the bureaucracy, and sought to expand Russian territory, especially in the Baltic region. Victory in the Great Northern War against Sweden expanded Russia’s influence, creating a formidable, centralized Russian state.

### Ottoman Absolutism

- **Distinctive Features of Ottoman Rule:** Unlike European absolutist states, the Ottoman Empire practiced religious tolerance through the millet system, which allowed religious communities to govern themselves. The empire’s governance was thus characterized by a decentralized approach, allowing autonomy within a diverse population.

### **Constitutionalism in England and the Dutch Republic (Ch. 15, Sec. 5)**

### Development of Constitutionalism in England

- **Foundation of Common Law:** Under Henry II, common law was established as a unified legal system across England, challenging the church's authority in legal matters and replacing regional laws with a centralized judicial structure.
- **Magna Carta (1215):** The Magna Carta, signed under King John, limited monarchical power and established basic legal rights, laying the groundwork for a government accountable to a set of laws.
- **Parliamentary Evolution:** Edward III and Henry VIII further empowered Parliament, calling it regularly to approve taxes and advise the king. Henry VIII used Parliament to break from the Catholic Church, establishing a precedent for the monarchy to consult with Parliament on matters of state.

### The English Civil War and its Consequences

- **Conflict between Charles I and Parliament:** Charles I’s belief in divine right led him to bypass Parliament and levy taxes independently, sparking resistance. His attempts to impose religious changes, including a merger of the English and Scottish churches, provoked a rebellion in Scotland. Parliament, summoned to provide funds for defense, clashed with Charles over power limits.
- **Civil War (1642–1651):** The war between Royalists (Cavaliers) and Parliamentarians (Roundheads) saw the rise of Oliver Cromwell, who led the New Model Army to victory. Charles I was captured, tried, and executed, a dramatic move that signaled a rejection of absolute monarchy.
- **The Protectorate and Cromwell’s Rule:** Cromwell established a military dictatorship under the Protectorate. He dissolved Parliament when it opposed his rule and divided England into military districts. Despite intentions of republican government, Cromwell’s authoritarian methods led to unrest, ultimately ending with his death and the restoration of the monarchy in 1660.

### The Glorious Revolution and Constitutional Monarchy

- **The Glorious Revolution (1688-1689):** Parliament invited William of Orange and his wife Mary to replace the Catholic James II, who promoted Catholicism and violated parliamentary privileges. William’s arrival led to a bloodless transfer of power, known as the Glorious Revolution.
- **Parliament’s Supremacy and the Bill of Rights:** The revolution cemented Parliament’s authority over the monarchy, with William and Mary agreeing to the English Bill of Rights, which limited the king’s power and secured Parliament’s role in governing. This marked the end of absolute monarchy and the establishment of a constitutional monarchy in England.

### Political Thought and John Locke’s Influence

- **John Locke’s Social Contract Theory:** Locke argued that government authority derived from the “consent of the governed.” Rulers were to protect citizens’ rights, and if they failed, citizens had the right to overthrow the government. Locke’s ideas challenged the concept of divine-right monarchy and justified constitutional government.
- **Contrast with Hobbes:** While Hobbes also theorized about a social contract, he supported an absolute ruler to maintain order. Locke, however, emphasized that rulers must respect citizens’ rights and uphold the social contract, influencing democratic principles in later governments.

### The Dutch Republic’s Political and Economic Structure

- **Decentralization in the Dutch Government:** The Dutch Republic was highly decentralized, with power held by wealthy provincial leaders and a strong focus on local governance. Each province managed its own affairs, ensuring citizen representation and a degree of autonomy within the republic.
- **Economic and Political Success:** The Dutch Republic’s economy thrived due to prioritization of trade and commerce. Dutch merchants held significant influence over government decisions, creating policies that encouraged international trade and economic growth, making the Netherlands one of Europe’s most prosperous nations.
